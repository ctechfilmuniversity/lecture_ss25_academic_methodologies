**INTRODUCTION**

The Turing Test, first introduced by Alan Turing in 1950, was designed to evaluate whether a machine can imitate human conversation well enough to be mistaken for a real person. It has since become a key method in the study of artificial intelligence (AI) (Livingstone, 2006). However, while the Turing Test focuses on general communication skills, it does not measure how well machines can express or respond to emotions. <br>
This paper argues that performance-based methods, especially from actor training like Stanislavski’s method, can help build a more precise way to test emotional communication in AI. In acting, _emotional believability_ is not judged by whether the actor truly feels something, but by whether their performance feels real to the audience. In a similar way, chatbots like GPT-Persona (_to be adapted which exactly is it at the end_) do not feel emotions, but they are trained to imitate emotional responses. These responses often appear correct on the surface but may lack emotional _depth_, such as clear motivation, subtext, or tone.<br>
This study focuses on the following question: _In what ways can the principles of subtext and emotional believability from actor training be adapted into a Turing Test framework to assess GPT-based chatbots in text-based affective scenarios?_
⁃ Emotional believability here means how well a response fits the emotional context, shows subtle emotional cues (subtext), and feels appropriate or human-like in tone and intention. <br>
To answer this, the following paper is divided into two main sections. First, it develops a theoretical framework based on Stanislavski’s acting principles, focusing on subtext. Second, it applies this framework in a small test using predefined prompts and chatbot responses, ((which will be rated by human evaluators)to be discussed). The goal is to explore how acting theory, can support the design of a more refined emotional Turing Test for AI.
